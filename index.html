The question

What we set out to test

AI coding tools stopped being autocomplete some time ago. They read repositories, edit files, run commands, call other tools, and check their own work. Working with one is a different skill from writing code.

The skills that matter now sit around the agent rather than inside it. Someone has to design the intent, expose the right context, decide what the agent is allowed to touch, and define what finished actually means. None of that is typing, and none of it is reviewing diffs.

The research is fairly consistent that these habits do not arrive with the tool. Developers using code-generation systems save effort in one place and spend it again on understanding, debugging and validating what came back (Vaithilingam et al., 2022; Mozannar et al., 2024). A survey of developers found the recurring complaint was not capability but control — getting output that satisfies real requirements (Liang et al., 2024).

The sharpest result is a security one. In a controlled experiment, participants given an AI assistant wrote less secure code than those without one, and were more confident that their code was secure (Perry et al., 2023). Tool access moved their behaviour and their confidence in opposite directions.

The productivity evidence pulls both ways, and it is worth being honest about that. Developers with GitHub Copilot finished a standardised task far faster than a control group (Peng et al., 2023). Experienced open-source maintainers working in repositories they knew well took longer with early-2025 tools than without them (Becker et al., 2025). % faster in Peng et al. (2023) · % slower in Becker et al. (2025), developers across tasks in mature repositories

Both findings are probably true. They describe different tasks, different codebases and different levels of practice with the tool. Which is exactly the gap this study sits in.

Access to a coding agent and proficiency with one are not the same thing.

So: can experienced engineers pick up the operational knowledge that agentic work requires from a short, structured program? Not whether they can be taught to code — they already can — but whether the specific habits of steering an agent transfer in two weeks. That is the question this evaluation was built to answer, and the honest answer turns out to be partial.

Item level

What moved, and what moved backwards

A single average hides almost everything interesting. Twelve questions moved in twelve different ways, and three of them moved the wrong way.

A net gain hides a lot of churn

Averages move smoothly. People do not. On the question about keeping repository guidance lean, thirteen participants who had it wrong got it right, and not one person went the other way — that is a clean, one-directional shift, and it is what learning is supposed to look like.

On the question about where durable team knowledge should live, four people got it right who had not before, and seven got it wrong who previously had. The net figure for that question is a small decline. The underlying movement is eleven people changing their minds, most of them away from the correct answer.

Q3 repository-guidance hygiene: improved, declined · Q12 durable source-of-truth context: improved, declined

This is the paper's real observation, and it is more useful than the headline number. Strategic understanding and operational judgement develop at different rates. If you have just taken the test, you probably felt it: the questions about what to believe were easy, and the questions about which mechanism to reach for were not.

The baseline paradox

The strongest starters barely moved

Split the cohort by where they started and the gains fall away almost perfectly as the starting score rises. That looks like a story about who benefits from training. It is mostly a story about the test.

The group that started at nine or above finished barely more than a question short of a perfect score. That is all the improvement the test had left to offer them — and some of even that was out of reach, because the two questions the whole cohort found hardest sit inside it. A flat line under those conditions is close to uninformative.

The lower-baseline group had six and a half questions of room and used about half of it. That is a real gain and the cleanest signal in the study. But the comparison between the groups is not a fair fight, because the instrument does not offer them the same distance to run.

This result should not be read as “experienced engineers gained nothing.” It should be read as: this test could not detect what they gained. Measuring advanced agentic practice needs a harder ruler than twelve multiple-choice items, and building one is the obvious next piece of work.

Why these three and not others

Look at what they have in common. Every one of them asks you to choose between adjacent plausible mechanisms. An MCP connector or a markdown file checked into the repository. The SDK, or a scheduled automation, or a packaged skill. A specialized agent role, or the default one with better prompting.

There is no silly option to eliminate. All four answers describe something a competent engineer might actually build, and three of them would half-work. The question is not whether you understand the principle — the cohort demonstrably did — but whether you know which lever this particular situation calls for.

Knowing the principle does not tell you which lever to pull.

That is the distinction the data keeps drawing. Strategic framing transferred in two weeks, and mechanism selection did not. It is also the part of agentic engineering that looks most like ordinary senior judgement: knowing which of several defensible tools fits the shape of the problem, which is learned mostly by doing it wrong a few times.

If you are designing a curriculum from this, the implication is fairly direct. The conceptual material can be taught quickly and probably does not need four live hours. The mechanism-selection material needs repetition against real cases, and two weeks was not enough of it.

Method

Inside the program

Two weeks, fully online, two modules, four live hours, and a required quiz at the end of each module.

1Two weeks, fully online Delivered to professional developers already working in engineering roles.
2Asynchronous materials Static study material available throughout, worked through at each participant's own pace.
3Four one-hour live sessions Two per module, run as concept → demonstration → practice → support, with live demos, guided exercises and troubleshooting.
4Module 1 — Codex foundations Setup, the agent loop, prompt and context design, planning, verification criteria, repository guidance, and common development workflows.
5Module 2 — advanced workflows Configuration, permissions and sandboxing, reusable skills, Model Context Protocol connections, automations, memory, parallel work, code review, and long-horizon execution.
6A required quiz per module Ten items each, pass required, retries allowed.
7The post-program assessment The identical twelve-item instrument, taken immediately after instruction and before anyone was given access to the capstone.
8A separate capstone week No new teaching was delivered during it. Capstone and hackathon outcomes are not analysed in the paper and are not discussed here.

Limitations

What this does not claim

Six of them, in rough order of how much they should change your reading.

No control group

This is the single largest limitation. Scores rose, and the program is the most plausible explanation, but nothing here rules out maturation, motivation, or anything else that happened to these engineers over the same two weeks. A controlled trial would say something this design cannot.

The same test, twice

Familiarity plausibly explains part of the speed gain and may have primed attention to particular topics during instruction. Stated fairly in the other direction: participants received no scores and no answer feedback after the pre-test, so familiarity alone does not explain newly correct answers on items they had previously missed.

The instrument was built for the curriculum

It was developed by Andela's internal Assessment Team and reviewed by subject-matter experts, which is a reasonable standard for program evaluation. It is not an independently validated research scale, and it was written by people who knew what was about to be taught.

Twelve questions is a short ruler

There is very little headroom for strong starters, which is why the flat higher-baseline line cannot carry much weight. It also makes every individual score coarse: one question is more than eight percentage points.

Knowledge, not performance

The study measures what participants could recognise and select in written scenarios. It does not measure whether they went on to ship better, safer or faster code. Those are the outcomes that matter, and this design does not reach them.

Exploratory by construction

The research question and the statistical framing were developed after the data was collected. Read this as program-evaluation evidence, not as causal proof, and treat the confidence interval as descriptive rather than confirmatory.

Why it matters

Procurement is not proficiency

Andela's work starts from a skills-first reading of the market: what someone can currently do matters more than where they trained or who they last worked for. Agentic tooling makes that reading harder and more urgent at the same time, because the relevant skill is new enough that almost nobody's résumé contains it.

That is the practical shape of talent debt. Organisations buy the licences, announce the rollout, and then discover that the gap between having the agent and getting value from it is a learning gap that nobody budgeted for. It shows up as inconsistent adoption, quiet abandonment, and a small number of enthusiasts whose habits never spread.

This study is a modest but concrete data point against that. Two weeks of structure moved assessed knowledge by a meaningful margin, and it moved it most for the people who started furthest behind. It did not move mechanism selection much, which tells you where a second pass should go.

Rolling out a coding agent is a procurement decision. Getting proficiency out of it is a learning decision. The two are routinely confused.

If you are funding one and not the other, this page is the argument for funding both.
